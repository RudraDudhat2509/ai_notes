CONCEPT 1: WHY CAPACITY ESTIMATION EXISTS
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Capacity estimation is the first 5 minutes of every system design interview.
Its purpose is NOT to get exact numbers. It's to force architecture decisions
grounded in data rather than instinct.

A system serving 100 req/s has a completely different architecture from one
serving 100,000 req/s. You can't skip this step — without numbers, every
design decision is a guess.

What an interviewer is watching for:
  1. Do you know how to translate product metrics into technical requirements?
  2. Do you know what numbers trigger architectural complexity?
  3. Can you reason from first principles, or do you just memorize patterns?

The output of estimation is not "I need 47.3 TB." It's:
  "Storage is in the PB range → distributed object storage (S3/GCS), not Postgres."
  "Writes are 50k/sec → Postgres is at its ceiling → consider Cassandra or Kafka."
  "Reads are 1M/sec → cache layer mandatory, read replicas needed."


━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CONCEPT 2: THE 7-STEP SKELETON
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Run through these in order, every time. Write numbers on the board as you go.

  Step 1 — WRITES/SECOND
    Formula:  daily_writes ÷ 10^5
    Why 10^5: 1 day = 86,400 seconds ≈ 100,000 = 10^5. This is your anchor.

  Step 2 — READS/SECOND
    Formula:  writes/s × read:write ratio

  Step 3 — SIZE PER EVENT
    Estimate KB or MB per individual record/event.
    Be explicit: "a tweet is ~280 chars text + metadata ≈ 1KB total."

  Step 4 — STORAGE PER DAY
    Formula:  writes/s × size_per_event × 86,400
    Or equivalently: daily_writes × size_per_event

  Step 5 — TOTAL STORAGE
    Formula:  storage/day × retention_in_days
    Retention: social apps (2-5 years), logs (30-90 days), messages (forever).

  Step 6 — BANDWIDTH (OUTBOUND)
    Formula:  reads/s × size_per_event
    This tells you if CDN is mandatory (>1 Gbps = almost always yes).

  Step 7 — SERVERS NEEDED
    Formula:  peak_req/s ÷ capacity_per_server
    Typical single server: 1,000-5,000 req/s for a web API. More for static files.

After each number: state what architecture decision it implies.
Don't just produce numbers. Produce decisions.

CONCEPT 3: THE MOST CRITICAL ANCHOR — 1 DAY ≈ 10^5 SECONDS
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

  1 day = 24 hours × 60 min × 60 sec = 86,400 seconds ≈ 100,000 = 10^5.

  NOT 10^6. That's 1,000,000 — off by 10×.

COMMON MISTAKE:
  "500M events/day ÷ 10^6 = 500 events/sec"  ← WRONG.
  "500M events/day ÷ 10^5 = 5,000 events/sec" ← RIGHT.

Dividing by 10^6 gives numbers that are 10× too small. Your architecture will
be wrong: you'll design for 500 req/s when you actually need to handle 5,000/s.

DRILL THIS UNTIL IT'S MUSCLE MEMORY:
  50M/day   → 500/sec
  100M/day  → 1,000/sec
  500M/day  → 5,000/sec
  1B/day    → 10,000/sec
  2B/day    → 20,000/sec
  10B/day   → 100,000/sec

SHORTCUT (for mental math):
  Step 1: Drop the "/day" unit.
  Step 2: Divide by 10^5 = move decimal 5 places left.
  Step 3: 500M = 5 × 10^8. Divide by 10^5 = 5 × 10^3 = 5,000.


━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CONCEPT 4: READ:WRITE RATIOS
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Read:write ratio is the multiplier from writes/s to reads/s.
Each product type has a characteristic ratio based on usage patterns.

  ┌──────────────────────────────────┬───────────┬────────────────────────────────────┐
  │ Product Type                     │ Ratio     │ Reasoning                          │
  ├──────────────────────────────────┼───────────┼────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ Social (Twitter, Instagram)      │ 100:1     │ 1 post read by 100s of followers   │
  │ Messaging (WhatsApp, iMessage)   │ 1:1       │ Each message sent is read once     │
  │ Video Streaming (YouTube)        │ 10:1      │ Few uploads, many views            │
  │ Ride-sharing (Uber, Ola)         │ 5:1       │ Location writes frequent, reads    │
  │                                  │           │ for matching + status updates      │
  │ Search engine                    │ 1000:1    │ Indexes updated rarely, queries    │
  │                                  │           │ constantly                         │
  │ Audit log / append-only          │ 1:10      │ Write-heavy: events flow in fast,  │
  │                                  │           │ queries are rare                   │
  └──────────────────────────────────┴───────────┴────────────────────────────────────┘

These ratios drive architecture:
  100:1 read-heavy → aggressive caching, read replicas, CDN.
  1:1 balanced → optimize both paths equally.
  Write-heavy (< 1:1) → Kafka for ingest, Cassandra or TimescaleDB for storage.

For group messaging: closer to N:1 where N = average group size (~5-10).
Still far below social media, but not quite 1:1.

CONCEPT 5: PEAK VS AVERAGE — ALWAYS DESIGN FOR PEAK
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Traffic is never flat. Step 1-2 give you AVERAGE load.
Your system must survive PEAK load. Apply a multiplier.

  Normal products (social, e-commerce): peak = 3× average.
    Morning and evening commutes, lunch breaks. Predictable daily pattern.

  Event-driven products (ticket sales, live streams, breaking news): peak = 10×.
    One tweet from a celebrity. One IPL match ending. One product launch.
    Your system goes from 0 to 10× in seconds. Must pre-provision or auto-scale.

WHY PEAK MATTERS:
  If average is 20k req/s and peak is 60k req/s (3×), design for 60k.
  If your system only handles average load and traffic spikes 3×:
    → Database overwhelmed → queries queue → latency explodes → timeouts
    → Users see errors → revenue loss → incident post-mortem.

EXAMPLE:
  Average QPS: 5,000.
  Peak multiplier: 3× → Design for 15,000 QPS.
  Server capacity: 5,000 req/s per server.
  Servers needed: 15,000 / 5,000 = 3 servers minimum. Add 1 buffer = 4 total.


━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CONCEPT 6: UNIT CONVERSION — STORAGE SIZES
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

  1 KB = 10^3 bytes     (1,000)
  1 MB = 10^6 bytes     (1,000,000)
  1 GB = 10^9 bytes     (1,000,000,000)
  1 TB = 10^12 bytes    (1,000,000,000,000)
  1 PB = 10^15 bytes    (1,000,000,000,000,000)

MOST COMMON MISTAKE: confusing TB and PB.
  500M users × 3MB/user = 500 × 10^6 × 3 × 10^6 = 1.5 × 10^15 bytes = 1.5 PB.
  NOT 1.5 TB. TB is 10^12. PB is 10^15. They differ by 1000×.

MENTAL MATH SHORTCUT:
  Multiply the user count and size first: 500M × 3MB = 1500M MB = 1500 TB.
  Then convert 1500 TB ÷ 1000 = 1.5 PB.

STORAGE OVERHEAD MULTIPLIERS (add to raw data size):
  Replication factor (distributed systems): 3× (3 copies for durability)
  Index storage: typically +20-30% of data size
  Metadata / overhead: +5-10%
  So raw estimate × 1.5 to 4× for real provisioned storage.

  Example: 1.5 PB raw data × 3 replicas = 4.5 PB provisioned storage.
  This is why S3 bills are larger than you expect.

CONCEPT 7: THE ARCHITECTURE DECISION TABLE
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Numbers from the skeleton feed directly into architecture decisions.

  ┌───────────────────────────────┬──────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │ Signal                        │ Architecture Implication                         │
  ├───────────────────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ Req/s < 1,000                 │ Single server, monolith is fine                  │
  │ Req/s 1k–10k                  │ Load balancer + 2-5 app servers                  │
  │ Req/s 10k–100k                │ Horizontal scaling, Redis cache mandatory         │
  │ Req/s > 100k                  │ CDN, edge caching, aggressive sharding           │
  ├───────────────────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ Storage < 1 TB                │ Single Postgres instance is fine                 │
  │ Storage 1–100 TB              │ Partitioned DB, possibly read replicas            │
  │ Storage > 100 TB              │ Distributed storage: S3 + Cassandra/HDFS         │
  │ Storage > 1 PB                │ Object storage (S3/GCS) is mandatory             │
  ├───────────────────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ Write rate < 5k/s             │ Postgres can handle it                           │
  │ Write rate 5k–50k/s           │ Postgres with partitioning, OR Kafka ingest      │
  │ Write rate > 50k/s            │ Cassandra or Kafka + time-series DB              │
  ├───────────────────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ Bandwidth > 1 Gbps            │ CDN is not optional                              │
  │ Media files (images/video)    │ Object storage (S3) + CDN, never in Postgres     │
  └───────────────────────────────┴──────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

WORKED EXAMPLE 1: URL SHORTENER
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Given: 100M new URLs shortened per day. Read:write = 10:1. 500 bytes per URL.
5 year retention. Peak multiplier 3×.

  Step 1 — Writes/sec:   100M ÷ 10^5 = 1,000 writes/sec
  Step 2 — Reads/sec:    1,000 × 10 = 10,000 reads/sec (average)
                         Peak reads = 10,000 × 3 = 30,000 reads/sec
  Step 3 — Size/event:   500 bytes
  Step 4 — Storage/day:  100M × 500B = 50 GB/day
  Step 5 — Total storage: 50 GB × 365 × 5 = 91 TB
  Step 6 — Bandwidth:    10,000 × 500B = 5 MB/sec = 40 Mbps (trivial)
  Step 7 — Servers:      30,000 req/s ÷ 5,000 req/s per server = 6 servers

DECISIONS:
  91 TB → partitioned Postgres (or Cassandra). Single node would struggle.
  10k-30k reads/sec → Redis cache mandatory (popular short URLs cached).
  5 MB/s bandwidth → CDN not needed for URLs themselves (tiny payloads).
  Redirect is the hot path → optimize for read latency (cache in Redis).

WORKED EXAMPLE 2: WHATSAPP MESSAGES
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Given: 1B users. Each sends 50 messages/day. Average message size 1KB.
Read:write = 1:1. Keep messages forever. Peak 3×.

  Step 1 — Writes/sec:   1B × 50 ÷ 10^5 = 50B ÷ 10^5 = 500,000/sec
  Step 2 — Reads/sec:    500,000 × 1 = 500,000/sec
                         Peak = 500,000 × 3 = 1.5M req/sec
  Step 3 — Size/event:   1KB
  Step 4 — Storage/day:  50B × 1KB = 50 TB/day
  Step 5 — Total storage (5 years): 50 TB × 365 × 5 = 91 PB
  Step 6 — Bandwidth:    500k × 1KB = 500 MB/sec = 4 Gbps
  Step 7 — Servers:      1.5M ÷ 5,000 = 300 servers

DECISIONS:
  500k writes/sec → Postgres is completely dead. Cassandra (built for this).
  91 PB storage → object storage + tiered archival (cold messages to cheaper tier).
  1.5M reads/sec → massive caching layer, sharded Redis cluster.
  4 Gbps bandwidth → CDN for media, but text messages are fine without.
  300 servers → auto-scaling groups, load balancers in front.

GATE QUESTIONS — VERIFY UNDERSTANDING
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Q1: Instagram has 500M users, each uploads 1 photo/day at 3MB. Daily storage?
A: 500M × 3MB = 500 × 10^6 × 3 × 10^6 = 1.5 × 10^15 bytes = 1.5 PB/day.
   NOT 1.5 TB. TB = 10^12, PB = 10^15. Off by 1000× if you confuse them.

Q2: A system gets 2B requests/day. Peak:average = 3×. Peak QPS?
A: Average = 2B ÷ 10^5 = 20,000 QPS. Peak = 20,000 × 3 = 60,000 QPS.

Q3: Chat app — what read:write ratio?
A: 1:1. Every message sent (write) is read once by recipient.
   Reads ≈ writes. Not 100:1 like social — only one person receives each message.

Q4: You estimate 10 TB of storage and 2,000 writes/sec. Which DB do you use?
A: 10 TB and 2,000/sec are both within Postgres's range (< 50 TB, < 5k writes/s).
   Single sharded Postgres or partitioned Postgres is fine. Cassandra overkill.

Q5: Your system needs to handle a sudden 10× traffic spike (new product launch).
    You designed for 3× peak. What happens and how do you prevent it?
A: At 10× you're 3× over capacity → cascading failures. Prevention:
   (1) Queue ingest (Kafka) to absorb bursts — consumers process at their own pace.
   (2) Circuit breakers to fail fast rather than cascade.
   (3) Auto-scaling with pre-warming (scale out 10 min before expected spike).
   (4) Shedding: rate limit non-critical features, degrade gracefully.
